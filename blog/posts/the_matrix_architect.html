UN Women, patriarchy and The Architect from The Matrix

How a fictional system reveals the hidden architectures shaping our real world

In March 2025, I had the privilege of attending CSW69 in a voluntary capacity as part of UN Women UK. Anyone who is interested can volunteer to take part in CSW ("Commission on the Status of Women"); it allows you to attend remotely any of hundreds of UN sessions where policy experts, researchers, activists, representatives from large NGOs and small community grass-roots organisations discuss challenges women are facing worldwide: economic challenges, disproportionate impacts of climate change, healthcare inequities, abuse, lack of representation and power within political, corporate, and scientific institutions, and more. And get an understanding on these topics from world experts to take back to our communities and workplaces.

CSW is completely overwhelming, mind‑blowing, fascinating and energising all at once — a mix of deeply frustrating inertia and lack of change in many political systems, combined with extraordinary achievements and progress from countless incredibly resourceful, brilliant, dedicated people in often underfunded, overstretched charities and community groups — and acute reminders that the inequalities still suffered by millions of women worldwide are completely unjust and will not improve unless we put in the work to make them improve.

One question I’ve been asked is: "Why do we need UN Women in a country like the UK? Shouldn't we focus on other countries where things are worse?"

Abi Claire Graham representing the UK at UN Women, CSW69, in March 2025.

It’s a fair question on the surface, especially in contexts where legal rights are broadly equal. I understand why people ask it, but the answer lies not in legislation or wide-spread (conscious) beliefs that women are worth less - but in deeply-rooted largely hidden social structures: in who designs the systems we live within, whose needs are considered and whose are not, who data exists about and who it doesn't, and whose voices are still considered the default — this absolutely impacts the lives of women in numerous ways in the UK, still having to "make do" with systems that weren’t designed for them (corporations, political systems, healthcare, even transport systems).

In fact anyone who isn't a middle/upper-class able-bodied straight cisgender white man tends to be adversely impacted by these systems and structures. It's actually very important in understanding these systems to consider the experiences of all women and gender-diverse people (all ethnicities, socioeconomic situations, disabled/non-disabled) and as a trans-inclusive intersectional global event, CSW does this.

It’s true that, in many ways, women in the UK are proportionately vastly more fortunate than women in many other parts of the world. And undoubtedly I'm having a much better time of it than my mother’s generation did, or the generation before hers. Progress has been made to happen by unrelenting efforts of mainly women in previous generations. But that progress doesn’t mean the work is done — and women everywhere, not just in the most unequal places, deserve and need equality.

And I genuinely believe that if our male friends, relatives, and colleagues fully recognised the inequalities that still shape the lives of their female loved ones — their partners, sisters, daughters, colleagues, friends — they would want to be part of changing that. I don't want to be unaware of inequalities that affect others I care about such as racism, socioeconomic inequality, homphobia, transphobia, I want to be in solidarity with people I care about and do what I can.

These systems are hidden from all of us, not just men - but I think women are a bit more aware of the numerous ways that gender-bias manifests, because they're more directly impacted, and because it's something women talk about more with other women. Men rarely get included in these conversations, partly because we don't want to make people feel uncomfortable, but it makes this reality even more invisible for men.

UN Women exists because, globally (yes — the UK included), women and girls continue to experience systemic inequality. From underrepresentation in leadership and overrepresentation in unpaid care work, to higher exposure to gender-based violence and economic vulnerability, the evidence remains stark.

In the UK, too, structural imbalances persist: men still dominate most decision-making roles across politics, business, media, and tech. Despite progress, gender remains one of the most consistent axes of power imbalance.

This isn't an academic abstract issue. Effects in the UK include:

  • Women having 40% less in their pension pot than men.
  • Only about 19% of UK companies are led by women, and female-led startups receive a disproportionately small proportion of funding — despite having businesses that offer better return-on-investment.
  • Add racism into that mix, and Black female founders suffer even greater inequities than white female founders in securing the investment they deserve.
  • Women disproportionately leave the tech industry annually (a consequence of various inequalities in company cultures and pay), costing the UK billions every year.
  • The online world is filled with misogyny, making it a particularly unpleasant, exclusionary and corrosive experience for many women.
  • Women spend around 25% more of their lives than men in debilitating ill health.
  • Women simply weren’t even included in many medical studies until the last couple of decades — so doctors just don’t have that much to go on when it comes to understanding women's health.
  • The consequences are far-reaching: women often experience more side effects from medications because dosages were based on male physiology.
  • 1 in 10 women have endometriosis, yet it takes nearly nine years on average to be diagnosed, leaving many to endure crippling pain for much of their lives. The condition is a serious economic and public-health problem, costing the UK economy an estimated £8.2 billion annually in healthcare expenses, lost work, and reduced productivity. (I myself suffered severe menstrual pain every month with no identified cause requiring a combination of prescription pain-killers and cocodamol, which brought with them unwanted side-effects.)
  • Symptoms of heart attacks in women are misunderstood or misdiagnosed because they present differently to men (and apparently are likely to have different root causes also). My own mother died aged 60 of heart failure from an unknown cause.
  • Plus, unconscious sexism is very prevalent in medical systems — with women (myself included) thought to be imagining or exaggerating real illnesses.
  • And it’s expensive being chronically ill. Women spend about 50% more than men on personal healthcare annually in the UK.
  • And we haven't even spoken about women's safety: the deep-rooted misogyny in many institutions, including for example the police, and even the unintentional gender bias in the way cities and transport systems are designed, making them particularly inefficient, costly, and unsafe for women...

And yet, many of these inequities and injustices are wholly invisible to a lot of people, none of this is self-evident.

How can we understand the hidden forces that are the source of these problems, and actually convince people that they're very real and need their personal interest and engagement for the sake of women in their own lives that they care about?

It was when I was wracking my brains on this one that a particular character from The Matrix trilogy just came into my mind...

The Architect as Patriarchy

The Architect is a character who appears in The Matrix Reloaded - he is a calculating, calm, clinical, emotionless white-bearded figure, and he is the designer of the Matrix itself. He speaks in complex, hyper-rational language and is the unseen force behind the digital world in which humans unknowingly live.

The Architect is clearly meant to be a deeply familiar classic patriarchal archetype — a cinematic distillation of centuries of elite male power: a symbol of how systems have been designed by a group of elite white men, around male perspectives, in ways that shape the lives of everyone else. His cold rationality often appears in our world as a supposedly benevolent neutral force, but it is often used in a way that is controlling and oppressive (whether intentionally or not) within many realms — the corporate world, and certainly within engineering and physics tech cultures where I've spent over half my life.

The Architect literally designs the world, much like how real-world systems (law, governance, media, education, medicine) have historically been designed by men, for men. The Matrix he creates is hegemonic, all-powerful and invisible — people live their whole lives trapped within it without realising it. This mirrors how gender norms and male-centred structures operate: subtly but profoundly shaping daily life while appearing “neutral.”

His emotional detachment and prioritisation of logic reflects the privileging of ‘masculine-coded’ traits like reason, control, and authority, while ‘feminine’ traits like empathy, intuition, and vulnerability are absent or devalued.

The Architect appears neutral, logical, even benevolent, while in fact only reflecting the priorities and perspectives of a historically dominant group.

The Architect: the emotionless designer of the Matrix, a deeply familiar archetype (from The Matrix Reloaded (2003), directed by The Wachowskis. © Warner Bros. Used under fair use for commentary and analysis.)

He has a distinguished white suit, a trimmed beard, he has a stern, calm controlled demeanor: he’s the visual embodiment of old-school male authority, like a professor, judge, or Victorian patriarch. His power is simply taken for granted; he doesn’t fight like the other characters, because he doesn’t need to. His dominance is structural, woven into the very code that creates the reality that humans sleep within.

This mirrors real-world dynamics, where male authority is often automatically, unconsciously treated as “default” or “neutral” in boardrooms, governments, and media, while women or gender-diverse people are expected to justify or prove themselves. His power is programmed into the Matrix, just as real-world patriarchy is embedded in the structures we’ve inherited: in legal systems, economic frameworks, media institutions, and knowledge production. We are all unconsciously programmed from our earliest years to assume that men like him have authority and expertise.

The Gender of Systems

The Architect represents the archetype of elite male authority — the system designer who appears objective, but whose objectivity is itself gendered. "Rational calm objectivity" is a lot easier when you control the world around you and serious deficiencies in this world simply don't much affect you personally. The aesthetic of The Architect evokes centuries of power: a Victorian judge, a philosopher-king, a bureaucrat of empire — an intense focus on order, efficiency, and a worldview wholly disconnected from the emotional, intuitive, and relational elements of human life.

Crucially, The Architect builds systems without consulting the people who will live within them. The humans in the Matrix are not collaborators in their own reality — they are subjects to be managed. The Architect observes them dispassionately, like lab rats, and decides — patriarchally, not paternalistically — what is "best" for them.

This is one of the most insidious qualities of patriarchy: it positions one group as the natural decision-makers for all, based on the belief that they know better.

The traits The Architect embodies — control, logic, detachment (supposedly born of rational objectivity but often a symptom of unawareness due to privilege) — have historically been coded as masculine and positioned as superior. Meanwhile, traits more often associated with the feminine (care, empathy, vulnerability) have not only been devalued or excluded from the design of public systems; they have also been strictly controlled under patriarchy, particularly within men.

From a very early age, boys are taught to suppress vulnerability, to distance themselves from softness, to fear being perceived as weak. The result is a form of emotional discipline so deep and internalised that many men spend their adult lives trying to understand how this conditioning has shaped their sense of self, their relationships, and their understanding of others (disproportionately leading to isolation). As bell hooks explores in The Will to Change, patriarchy does not liberate men — it emotionally constrains them, often with profound consequences.

In contrast to The Architect, the Matrix universe also gives us The Oracle — a warm, maternal, intuitive foil to The Architect’s cold precision. This binary isn’t neutral either. It reinforces an old pattern: reason versus feeling, male versus female, structure versus chaos.

Real-World Echoes

The Architect isn’t based on one man, but on a lineage of power. He echoes numerous historical figures including:

  • Sigmund Freud, whose theories shaped psychology through a distinctly male lens.
  • Le Corbusier, the iconic modernist architect whose rational city plans (like those of many architects of his time) have been widely criticised for overlooking lived human, and particularly gendered, needs.
  • Plato, whose philosopher-kings privileged rationality over lived experience.
  • Colonial administrators, governing from afar with bureaucratic detachment.
  • Early computer scientists, who designed the digital world from within overwhelmingly male paradigms.

By placing figures like Freud, Plato, or Le Corbusier side by side, I’m not claiming they shared intentions or meant harm. I’m using them as cultural symbols of a long lineage of elite male rationalist authority - one that leads us to unconsciously assume male credibility and expertise - and as examples of how patriarchal norms inevitably shape even the most brilliant minds in ways that we're usually unaware of.

To a greater or lesser extent, men automatically walk through our world unconsciously triggering our deeply conditioned assumptions that men are credible, rational, objective authorities and leaders. An effect which is amplified the more a man looks and sounds like The Architect — white, distinguished, elite. There are other powerful male archetypes too that automatically gain respect because we've seen them repeated so many times in our culture, such as the “troubled genius” eccentric professor type — dangerous because we even assume that some volatile bad behaviour might inherently accompany genius, so we should overlook it.

Unfortunately, extensive research has proven that we really do unconsciously react to middle- or upper-class white men as credible and authoritative - even when we don’t particularly like them. We often assume their competence until it’s disproven, and we even overlook mistakes. By contrast, white women and anyone outside that narrow demographic tend to be scrutinised more closely, because we assume they will not be credible, we actively look for mistakes, and they must continually re-prove themselves.

So what does that do to a person, to walk through their whole life being automatically treated as though they have credibility and competence? A certain level of confidence or even overconfidence perhaps?

And conversely, what does that do to somebody walking through life who's automatically assumed in every situation not to have credibility or competence, but to have to prove it again and again, no matter how much experience they have? Might they perhaps lack confidence as as result? After all, women do get told quite often that they "just need to be more confident", and research shows women experience so-called "imposter syndrome" more often than men...

Incidentally, both trans men and trans women consistently confirm that being treated as competent/credible or not based on whether we appear male or female is a very real effect. - and they're in the unique position of knowing what both experiences feel like.

The real-world personal costs of these systems? Pay disparities, disparities in career progression, significantly smaller pensions, serious disparities in medical knowledge, chronic ill health but being disbelieved by doctors, being overlooked by investors, and so on.

Why This Still Matters

Even today in the UK, men’s perspectives remain centred by default:

  • Men still hold the majority of senior roles in politics, business, and academia.
  • Most editors, directors, and on-screen protagonists in UK media are male.
  • Only around 15–23% of senior venture-capital decision-makers in the UK are women, leaving investment decisions overwhelmingly male — a key factor in women-led startups receiving only a tiny fraction of total funding (with outcomes worse again, by around a factor of four, for Black female founders).
  • Medical research continues to focus on male bodies, leading to diagnostic gaps for women.
  • Urban design still prioritises patterns aligned with male routines and assumptions.

This isn’t about individual men. These systems already existed before any of us were born; we just inherit them — but then allow the systems to continue to replicate themselves unless we recognise the need to consciously rebuild them.

Conclusion: We All Live in the Matrix

If someone asks “Why do we need UN Women?”, the goal is not to elevate women above men. It’s to dismantle the hidden architectures that have, for centuries, positioned one type of person as the norm — the centre of our societies and corporations — and positioned everyone else as a deviation, a "niche" lesser interest.

To escape these systems, we have to become aware of and build the capability to see the code that these systems operate from — the patriarchal patterns — and start to recognise the way that these patterns persist because we unconsciously continue to power them.

Well - that all sounds rather overwhelming doesn't it?! Powerful systems that are built into everything, that began long before we were all born, that are by default invisible to us?? What can we do? What can men do? First of all - all of us actually have rather more power than we think in the world immediately around us. We have significant power to raise others' awareness and our own - and this in itself is a powerful act.

Because when men choose to invest that effort in learning about the bias of systems that benefit them in ways they never chose, they can use that relative power by joining the fight - by looking at their own workplaces and making it clear that educating ourselves in this really matters - for real people in our immediate vicinity. Unconscious bias inevitably prevails in a multitude of ways when we are unaware, and do not recognise what kinds of policies can interrupt it day to day. Also - by disengaging from these topics, that's actually compounding the existing effect of women's needs typically not being given equal consideration, and women themselves unconsciously not being treated as carrying the same importance.

A very understandable reaction that men often have when gender-bias is discussed is to debate it - afterall it operates in hidden ways, so our common sense tells us it surely can't be a significant problem? But debating women directly on this actually serves to very seriously reinforce the gender-biased effect of not being viewed as rational, or credible - and it's unfair. It's a bit equivalent to debating someone suffering from a hidden medical condition about whether it's real or not and asking them to somehow prove it. We don't need to ask individuals to prove these things and we shouldn't - we can just learn about it from the thousands of books that have already been written. Books like "Invisible Women" by Caroline Criado Perez are a great place to start.

It's also worth noting - many women are initially unaware of how these disadvantages manifest. How many women have reams of statistics at their finger-tips about gender-bias, or have had the time or capacity to go through complex research in a field they haven't studied? But I've sometimes seen men asking individual women whether they've been affected by gender-bias, and taking an answer of "no" at face value - but the answer "yes" is in my experience less likely to be taken at face value. Most of us simply have no way to directly know how our lives might have been different had we been of the oppostive gender. But many decades of research tells us in broad terms the way that bias leads to the irrefutable statistical facts about inequality that I shared earlier in this blog - as do the incredibly impactful personal accounts of trans people, who can tell us how they were perceived and treated before and after a transition.

And the thing is, once men are aware of these topics, that carries great power with other men - because we find men speaking on these topics more believable than women!!! Now I realise that most men will feel anything but powerful, persuasive or authorative, and find this topic completely overwhelming - this is not a quick fix kind of thing though, it takes slow continous work, but I'd also say you already hold a power that you don't realise you have. Conversely, if men don't join in, then women are left alone in the fight - with substantially less influence overall to affect change, whilst living with the disadvantages of these systems, which is really quite lonely. Men simply showing visible sustained interest in this, and investing time to learn matters on a deeply personal level - that alone makes a profound difference in our immediate relationships. At work, or in friendships and family.

So let us undo the Matrix world, and live in a new real world together where we do not sleep through inequalities affecting people closest to us, a world of true solidarity, justice, and true human connection for all.
