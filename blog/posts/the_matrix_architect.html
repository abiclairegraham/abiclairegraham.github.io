UN Women, patriarchy and The Architect from The Matrix

The Matrix series are some of my absolutely favourite films. I think I must have seen The Matrix (1999) at least 10 times now - but I found it an absolute revelation when I learned relatively recently that the first Matrix film is intended to be an allegory for the experience of transgender people. I've got a few thoughts on that, which I'll put in a separate article. But suffice to say that themes of social systems and gender are very strong in The Matrix films in general. So what follows here are reflections that were prompted by my participation in the UN "Commission on the Status of Women" in 2025 and what I think is the incredibly powerful connection between a Matrix character called The Architect, and the systems of patriarchy which underlie persistent gender-inequality worldwide.

In March 2025, I had the privilege of attending CSW69 in a voluntary capacity as part of UN Women UK. Anyone who is interested can volunteer to take part in CSW ("Commission on the Status of Women"); it allows you to attend remotely any of hundreds of UN sessions where policy experts, researchers, activists, representatives from large NGOs and small community grass-roots organisations discuss challenges women are facing worldwide: economic challenges, disproportionate impacts of climate change, healthcare inequities, abuse, lack of representation and power within political, corporate, and scientific institutions, and more. And get an understanding on these topics from world experts to take back to our communities and workplaces.

CSW is completely overwhelming, mind‑blowing, fascinating and energising all at once — a mix of deeply frustrating inertia and lack of change in many political systems, combined with extraordinary achievements and progress from countless incredibly resourceful, brilliant, dedicated people in often underfunded, overstretched charities and community groups — and acute reminders that the inequalities still suffered by millions of women worldwide are completely unjust and will not improve unless we put in the work to make them improve.

One question I’ve been asked is: "Why do we need UN Women in a country like the UK? Shouldn't we focus on other countries where things are worse?"

Abi Claire Graham representing the UK at UN Women, CSW69, in March 2025.

It’s a fair question on the surface, especially in contexts where legal rights are broadly equal. I understand why people ask it, but the answer lies not in legislation or wide-spread (conscious) beliefs that women are worth less - but in deeply-rooted largely hidden social structures: in who designs the systems we live within, whose needs are considered and whose are not, who data exists about and who it doesn't, and whose voices are still considered the default — this absolutely impacts the lives of women in numerous ways in the UK, still having to "make do" with systems that weren’t designed for them (corporations, political systems, healthcare, even transport systems).

In fact anyone who isn't a middle/upper-class able-bodied straight cisgender white man tends to be adversely impacted by these systems and structures. It's actually very important in understanding these systems to consider the experiences of all women and gender-diverse people (all ethnicities, socioeconomic situations, disabled/non-disabled) and as a trans-inclusive intersectional global event, CSW does this.

It’s true that, in many ways, women in the UK are proportionately vastly more fortunate than women in many other parts of the world. And undoubtedly I'm having a much better time of it than my mother’s generation did, or the generation before hers. Progress has been made to happen by unrelenting efforts of mainly women in previous generations. But that progress doesn’t mean the work is done — and women everywhere, not just in the most unequal places, deserve and need equality.

And I genuinely believe that if our male friends, relatives, and colleagues fully recognised the inequalities that still shape the lives of their female loved ones — their partners, sisters, daughters, colleagues, friends — they would want to be part of changing that. I don't want to be unaware of inequalities that affect others I care about such as racism, socioeconomic inequality, homphobia, transphobia, I want to be in solidarity with people I care about and do what I can.

These systems are hidden from all of us, not just men - but I think women are a bit more aware of the numerous ways that gender-bias manifests, because they're more directly impacted, and because it's something women talk about more with other women. Men rarely get included in these conversations, partly because we don't want to make people feel uncomfortable, but it makes this reality even more invisible for men.

UN Women exists because, globally (yes — the UK included), women and girls continue to experience systemic inequality. From underrepresentation in leadership and overrepresentation in unpaid care work, to higher exposure to gender-based violence and economic vulnerability, the evidence remains stark.

In the UK, too, structural imbalances persist: men still dominate most decision-making roles across politics, business, media, and tech. Despite progress, gender remains one of the most consistent axes of power imbalance.

This isn't an academic abstract issue. Effects in the UK include:

  • Women having 40% less in their pension pot than men.
  • Only about 19% of UK companies are led by women, and female-led startups receive a disproportionately small proportion of funding — despite having businesses that offer better return-on-investment.
  • Add racism into that mix, and Black female founders suffer even greater inequities than white female founders in securing the investment they deserve.
  • Women disproportionately leave the tech industry annually (a consequence of various inequalities in company cultures and pay), costing the UK billions every year.
  • The online world is filled with misogyny, making it a particularly unpleasant, exclusionary and corrosive experience for many women.
  • Women spend around 25% more of their lives than men in debilitating ill health.
  • Women simply weren’t even included in many medical studies until the last couple of decades — so doctors just don’t have that much to go on when it comes to understanding women's health.
  • The consequences are far-reaching: women often experience more side effects from medications because dosages were based on male physiology.
  • 1 in 10 women have endometriosis, yet it takes nearly nine years on average to be diagnosed, leaving many to endure crippling pain for much of their lives. The condition is a serious economic and public-health problem, costing the UK economy an estimated £8.2 billion annually in healthcare expenses, lost work, and reduced productivity. (I myself suffered severe menstrual pain every month with no identified cause requiring a combination of prescription pain-killers and cocodamol, which brought with them unwanted side-effects.)
  • Symptoms of heart attacks in women are misunderstood or misdiagnosed because they present differently to men (and apparently are likely to have different root causes also). My own mother died aged 60 of heart failure from an unknown cause.
  • Plus, unconscious sexism is very prevalent in medical systems — with women (myself included) thought to be imagining or exaggerating real illnesses.
  • And it’s expensive being chronically ill. Women spend about 50% more than men on personal healthcare annually in the UK.
  • And we haven't even spoken about women's safety: the deep-rooted misogyny in many institutions, including for example the police, and even the unintentional gender bias in the way cities and transport systems are designed, making them particularly inefficient, costly, and unsafe for women...

And yet, many of these inequities and injustices are wholly invisible to a lot of people, none of this is self-evident.

How can we understand the hidden forces that are the source of these problems, and actually convince people that they're very real and need their personal interest and engagement for the sake of women in their own lives that they care about?

It was when I was wracking my brains on this one that a particular character from The Matrix trilogy just came into my mind...

The Architect as Patriarchy

The Architect is a character who appears in The Matrix Reloaded. He is a calculating, calm, clinical, emotionless white-bearded figure, and he is the designer of the Matrix itself. He speaks in complex, hyper-rational language and is the unseen force behind the digital world in which humans unknowingly live.

The Architect is clearly meant to be a deeply familiar patriarchal archetype: a cinematic distillation of centuries of elite male power. He symbolises how systems have been designed by groups of elite white men, around male perspectives, in ways that shape the lives of everyone else. His cold rationality often appears in our world as a supposedly neutral or benevolent force, but in practice it frequently functions as something controlling or oppressive (whether intentionally or not). This is certainly familiar within the corporate world, and within engineering and physics tech cultures where I have spent much of my life.

He literally designs the world, much like how real-world systems — law, governance, media, education, medicine — have historically been designed by men, for men. The Matrix he creates is hegemonic, all-powerful and invisible. People live their whole lives inside it without realising it. This mirrors how gender norms and male-centred structures operate: subtly but profoundly shaping daily life while appearing “neutral”.

The Architect: the emotionless designer of the Matrix, a deeply familiar archetype (from The Matrix Reloaded (2003), directed by The Wachowskis. © Warner Bros. Used under fair use for commentary and analysis.)

The Architect appears neutral, logical and even benevolent, but in reality he only reflects the priorities and perspectives of a historically dominant group. His emotional detachment and prioritisation of logic reflect the privileging of masculine-coded traits like reason, control and authority, while feminine-coded traits such as empathy, intuition and vulnerability are absent or devalued.

Visually, his distinguished white suit, trimmed beard and calm, stern demeanour embody an old-school form of male authority — the professor, the judge, the Victorian patriarch. His power is simply taken for granted. He does not fight like the other characters because he does not need to. His dominance is structural, woven into the very code that creates the reality humans sleep within. This mirrors real-world dynamics, where male authority is often automatically treated as the default in boardrooms, governments and media, while women or gender-diverse people are expected to justify their presence and prove their credibility. Just as the Matrix is programmed with his worldview, real-world patriarchy is embedded in the structures we inherit and often unconsciously reproduce.

The Gender of Systems

The Architect represents the archetype of elite male authority: the system designer who appears objective, though his objectivity is itself gender-biased in how it views the world, and in the impacts of his work. Rational calm objectivity is far easier to perform when you control the world around you, and when the world’s deficiencies rarely affect you personally. And lets be clear - in a human mind, rationality logic and objecitvity are absolutely performances, and definitely not innate traits. Patriarchy makes us incorrectly believe that rationality is innnately found in men. The aesthetic of the Architect evokes centuries of power — a Victorian judge, a philosopher-king, a bureaucrat of empire — with their intense focus on order, efficiency and a worldview disconnected from emotional, intuitive and relational aspects of human life.

Crucially, The Architect builds systems without consulting the people who will live within them. Humans in the Matrix are not collaborators in their own reality; they are subjects to be managed. He observes them dispassionately, like lab rats, and decides what is “best” (patriarchally, not paternalistically). This is one of patriarchy’s most insidious qualities: positioning one group as the natural decision-makers for all, based on the often unconscious belief that they inherently know better.

The traits The Architect embodies — control, logic, detachment (appearing to stem from rational objectivity but often born of privilege and unawareness) — have historically been coded as masculine and positioned as superior. Meanwhile, traits commonly associated with the feminine — care, empathy, vulnerability — have been devalued or excluded from the design of public systems, and tightly controlled within men.

From a very early age, boys are taught to suppress vulnerability, to distance themselves from softness and to fear being perceived as weak. The result is a form of emotional discipline so deep and internalised that many men spend their adult lives trying to understand how this conditioning has shaped their sense of self, their relationships and their understanding of others. It often leads to profound isolation. As bell hooks explores in The Will to Change, patriarchy does not liberate men; it emotionally constrains them with serious consequences.

The Architect’s clinical, managerial view of human beings also strongly echoes the "logic" of colonialism. He clearly views humans as pathetic, deeply inferior beings, and talks of losing "entire crops" of humans in earlier Matrix iterations. His interest in humans is simply how to keep them alive in order to generate power for The Matrix. This also parallels the strong link between transatlantic slavery and capitalism, where enslaved humans were the "engine" that powered the industrial revolution.

The detached, “benevolent”, hyper-rational authority of The Architect mirrors the rhetoric European empires used to justify domination: that they alone knew what was “best,” and that others required their governance or improvement. Colonial powers framed exploitation, control and even enslavement as a civilising mission. This mindset grew out of the long-standing patriarchal worldview that normalised hierarchical domination, and long positioned elite men as the natural arbiters of reason and order. The Architect embodies this fusion of patriarchal and colonial authority: a figure who designs a world for others without their consent, while believing himself impeccably logical and benevolent.

In contrast to The Architect, the Matrix universe also gives us The Oracle — a warm, maternal, intuitive foil to his cold precision. It reinforces a classic false binary and false dichotomy: reason versus feeling, male versus female, structure versus chaos. The Oracle also happens to be a Black woman, living in a deprived-looking urban tower-block, so is also "opposite" in social-class and race.

Real-World Echoes

The Architect isn’t based on one man, but on a long lineage of power. He echoes countless historical figures including:

  • Sigmund Freud, whose theories shaped psychology through a distinctly male lens.
  • Le Corbusier, the iconic modernist architect whose rational city plans (like those of many architects of his time) have been widely criticised for overlooking lived human, and particularly gendered, needs.
  • Plato, whose philosopher-kings privileged rationality over lived experience.
  • Colonial administrators, governing from afar with bureaucratic detachment.
  • Early computer scientists, who designed the digital world from within overwhelmingly male paradigms.

By putting figures like Freud, Plato, or Le Corbusier in the same list, I’m not claiming they shared intentions or meant harm. I’m using them as cultural symbols of a long lineage of elite male rationalist authority - one that still leads us to unconsciously assume male credibility and expertise - and as examples of how patriarchal norms inevitably shape even the most brilliant minds in ways that we're usually unaware of.

To a greater or lesser extent, men automatically walk through our world unconsciously triggering our deeply conditioned assumptions that men are credible, rational, objective authorities and leaders - an effect which is amplified the more a man looks and sounds like The Architect — white, distinguished, elite. These are assumptions that have been programmed into us through repeated exposure to exactly the types of male authorities I listed above (side-note - in The Matrix, I think this conditioning is what is symbolised by the feeding of the liquified dead to the sleeping newborn babies in the Matrix). There are other powerful male archetypes too that automatically gain respect because we've seen them repeated so many times in our culture, such as the “troubled genius” eccentric professor type — dangerous because we even assume that some volatile bad behaviour might inherently accompany genius, so we should overlook it.

Unfortunately, extensive research has proven that we really do unconsciously react to middle- or upper-class white men as credible and authoritative - even when we don’t particularly like them. We often assume their competence until it’s disproven, and we even overlook mistakes. By contrast, white women and anyone outside that narrow demographic tend to be scrutinised more closely, because we assume they will not be credible, we actively look for mistakes, and they must continually re-prove themselves.

So what does that do to a person, to walk through their whole life being automatically treated as though they have credibility and competence? A certain level of confidence or even overconfidence perhaps?

And conversely, what does that do to somebody walking through life who's automatically assumed in every situation not to have credibility or competence, but to have to prove it again and again, no matter how much experience they have? Might they perhaps lack confidence as as result? After all, women do get told quite often that they "just need to be more confident", and research shows women experience so-called "imposter syndrome" more often than men...

Incidentally, both trans men and trans women consistently confirm that being treated as competent/credible or not based on whether we appear male or female is a very real effect. - and they're in the unique position of knowing what both experiences feel like.

So what are the real-world personal costs to women of these patriarchal systems? Pay disparities, disparities in career progression, significantly smaller pensions, being overlooked by investors, serious disparities in medical knowledge, elevated rates of chronic ill health but being disbelieved by doctors, and so on.

Why This Still Matters

Even today in the UK, men’s perspectives remain centred by default:

  • Men still hold the majority of senior roles in politics, business, and academia.
  • Most editors, directors, and on-screen protagonists in UK media are male.
  • Only around 15–23% of senior venture-capital decision-makers in the UK are women, leaving investment decisions overwhelmingly male — a key factor in women-led startups receiving only a tiny fraction of total funding (with outcomes worse again, by around a factor of four, for Black female founders).
  • Medical research continues to focus on male bodies, leading to diagnostic gaps for women.
  • Urban design still prioritises patterns aligned with male routines and assumptions.

However, just as The Architect is specifically an upper-class white man, the men who overwhelmingly hold the most power are upper-class and white. This does not however erase the benefits that patriarchy conveys for other men - these still hold in relative terms compared to women of otherwise similar demographic.

However - compared to women of any other demographic, white (non-disabled, cisgender, middle/upper class women) carry a lot of relative power. Social disadvantages tend to compound, which is referred to as intersectionality.

Gender inequality manifests differently according to socioeconomic class - for example, historically, winning the right to work was never an issue for working-class or Black women, who always had to work, and white women were allowed to vote decades before Black women (see Women, Race and Class by Angela Davis for more on this). In modern-day UK, Women already carry a disproportionate burden of unpaid care work — childcare, elder care, domestic labour — which limits their time, restricts career opportunities, and reduces lifelong earnings. This creates financial vulnerability, especially for women with dependants. For women in socio-economically deprived situations who lack financial independence, leaving exploitative workplaces or abusive relationships can be extremely difficult, thereby compounding the harm of patriarchy.

For Black women, these inequalities are compounded by systemic racism. Black people in the UK are disproportionately affected by poverty, and this economic disadvantage hits Black women hardest. Alongside lower pay and heavier care burdens, Black women inescapably face the daily toll of racism in workplaces, social situations, healthcare systems and other institutions, which affects mental health, safety, and access to opportunity. Patriarchal systems are already largely invisible to men even though women are 50% of the population, so because the Black population is relatively small in the UK (and highly concentrated in certain geographical areas), most white people simply never witness the realities Black communities face. This leaves Black women in a very isolated unsupported position, contending with sexism, racism, and statistically also greater financial insecurity, often with far too few people they can trust or who truly understand what they are navigating.

Therefore, gender inequality is deeply intertwined with class, race and economic power, creating compounded barriers for the women most in need of support.

So, patriarchy isn’t about individual men. These systems already existed before any of us were born; we just inherit them — but then allow the systems to continue to replicate themselves unless we recognise the need to consciously rebuild them.

Conclusion: We All Live in the Matrix

If someone asks “Why do we need UN Women?”, the goal is not to elevate women above men. It is to dismantle the hidden architectures that have, for centuries, positioned one type of person as the norm, the centre of our societies and corporations, while positioning everyone else as a deviation or a “niche” lesser interest.

To escape these systems, we have to learn to see the code they run on; the patriarchal patterns beneath the surface. We also need to recognise the ways we unconsciously continue to power them.

That can feel overwhelming. These are powerful structures that began long before any of us were born, and they are largely invisible. So what can we do? What can men do? First, it is worth remembering that all of us have far more influence than we realise in the worlds immediately around us: our workplaces, friendships, families, and communities. Simply becoming curious, raising awareness, and choosing to learn are powerful acts.

A very understandable reaction men often have when gender bias is discussed is to question or debate it. This is natural, because gender bias operates in hidden ways, and our common sense tells us it surely cannot be that significant. Yet debating women directly about whether sexism in the UK is “real” can unintentionally reinforce the very dynamic we are trying to challenge: the assumption that women are less credible or rational. It is a bit like asking someone with a hidden medical condition to prove their symptoms. We do not need individual women to prove anything, because the evidence already exists in decades of research in the form of statistics and the causal mechanisms. Books like Invisible Women by Caroline Criado Perez are a great place to start.

It is also worth remembering that many women are initially unaware of how bias has shaped their own lives. Most of us do not walk around with research statistics at our fingertips, and none of us can know how our lives might have unfolded differently had we been another gender. Yet the research does show clear patterns. These patterns are supported not only by data but also by the powerful personal accounts of trans people, who have lived on both sides of society’s gendered expectations.

Patriarchy, capitalism and white supremacy do not only harm the people they marginalise however. They also compromise the well-being of the men they simultaneously convey benefits upon that were not asked for. These systems condition us to internalise that the worth of men lies in status, productivity, self-sufficiency and relentless resilience. Many men end up overworked, emotionally isolated and disconnected from the very relationships that would sustain them. And because patriarchal norms reward toughness and punish vulnerability with a loss of status, in combination with the economic demands that are on all of us, men can become trapped in patterns that exhaust them, leaving little bandwidth to challenge the patriarchal systems that created those patterns in the first place.

I've definitely come to believe that one of the most powerful anti-patriarchal steps a man can take is to reflect on his own care and well-being, and to believe that he deserves rest, connection and a sense of worth that is not tied to performance - and to very bravely role-model this for other men. But fundamentally, a man's humanity is what matters most to the people who care about him. Reclaiming this humanity is not a distraction from dismantling patriarchy; it is part of dismantling it, because nurturing human connection is exactly what is needed to create healthy workplaces and societies where everyone is seen, where everyone knows their worth and where everyone can thrive.

I wonder also if a reason this topic of patriarchy and gender-bias feels so overwhelming to men is because it's asking them to to take part in solving a problem that, within our lifetimes anyway, will be nowhere near resolved. And I think this perhaps creates a sense of helplessness, maybe more in men than in women. Taking part in our collective work to make these systems better is something of an act of faith because it's not that easy to see the tangible results of our efforts. So it's not a kind of work that gets much in the way of status or direct recognition. And again, I think that's quite hard - maybe particularly for men, because it most likely will feel that they haven't really "done" anything of substance.

Crucially however - once men gain some understanding on these topics, that knowledge carries enormous weight with other men, because research shows we tend to find men’s voices more believable than women’s. Most men do not feel powerful or authoritative when talking about gender bias, but I promise that you already have more influence than you think. And if men do not join in, women are left to push for change alone, with far less social power, while also living with the real harm of these systems. Simply showing visible, sustained interest in these issues is, in itself, an immediate antidote to patriarchal conditioning, which encourages men to prioritise individual experience and self-sufficiency over collective awareness and care. Choosing curiosity and solidarity makes a profound and very immediate difference in your closest relationships and communities, making relationships more whole - because how can relationships be whole if we're not fighting for a better world for those right beside us.

So let us wake up from the Matrix world, and live in a new real world together in true solidarity where we do not sleep through inequalities affecting the people closest to us, a world of justice, awareness, wellbeing and true human connection for all.
