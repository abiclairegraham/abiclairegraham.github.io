The Architect and the Algorithm: What The Matrix Can Teach Us About Patriarchy

In March 2025, I had the privilege of attending CSW69 in a voluntary capacity as part of UN Women UK. Anyone who is interested can volunteer to attend CSW, it allows you to attend remotely any of hundreds of UN sessions where policy experts, researchers, activists, representatives from large NGOs and small commmunity grass-roots organisations discuss challenges women are facing worldwide, economic challenges, disproportionate impacts of climate change, healhcare inequities, abuse, lack of representaiton and power within political, corporate, scientific institutions and more.

It's completely overwhelming, mind-blowing, fascinating and energising all at once. A mix of deeply frustrating lack of change in many political systems, combined with extraordinary achievements and progress from countless incredibly resourceful, brilliant, dedicated people in often underfunded, overstretched charities, community groups - and acute reminders that the inequalities still suffered by millions of women worldwide are completely unjust and will not improve unless we put in the work to make them improve.

One question I’ve been asked is: "Why do we need UN Women in a country like the UK? Shouldn't we focus on other countries where things are worse?"

Abi Claire Graham representing the UK at UN Women, CSW69, in March 2025.

It’s a fair question on the surface, especially in contexts where legal rights are broadly equal. But the answer lies not in legislation, but in structures: in who designs the systems we live within, whose needs are centred, and whose voices are still considered the default - this absolutely impacts on the lives of women in numerous ways in the UK, still having to "make do" with systems that were't designed for them (corporations, political systems, healthcare, even transport systems).

UN Women exists because, globally (yes - the UK included), women and girls continue to experience systemic inequality. From underrepresentation in leadership and overrepresentation in unpaid care work, to higher exposure to gender-based violence and economic vulnerability, the evidence remains stark.

In the UK, too, structural imbalances persist: men still dominate most decision-making roles across politics, business, media, and tech. Despite progress, gender remains one of the most consistent axes of power imbalance.

This isn't an academic abstract issue. Effects in the UK include:

  • Women having 40% less in their pension-pot than men.
  • Only about 19% of UK companies are led by women, and female-led startups receive a disproportionately small proportion of funding - despite having businesses that off better return-on-investment.
  • Add racism into that mix, and black female founders suffer even greater inequities than white female founders in securing the investment they deserve.
  • Women disproportionately leave the tech industry annually (a consequence of various inequalities in company cultures and pay), costing the UK billions every year.
  • The online world is filled with misogyny, making it a particularly unpleasant and corrosive experience for many women.
  • Women spending around 25% more of their lives than men in debilitating health.
  • Women simply weren't even included in a lot of medical studies until the last couple of decades - so doctors just don't have that much to go on when to comes to understanding women's health.
  • The consequences are far-reaching but women often experience more side effects from medications because dosages were based on male physiology.
  • Also, symptoms of heart attacks in women are misunderstood or misdiagnosed because they present differently to in men (and apparently are likely to have different root-causes also). My own mother died aged 60 of heart-failure from an unknown cause.
  • Plus, unconscious sexism is very prevalent in medical systems - with women (myself included) thought to be imagining or exaggerating real illnesses.
  • And it's expensive being chronically-ill. Women spend about 50% more than men on personal healthcare annually in the UK.
  • And we haven't even spoken about women's safety, the deep-rooted misogyny in many institutions including for example the police, even the unintentional gender-bias in the way cities and transport-systems are designed making them particularly inefficient, costly and unsafe for women...

And yet, many of these inequities and injustices are wholly invisible to a lot of people.

How can we understand the hidden forces that are the source of these problems, and actually convince people that they're very real and need their personal interest and engagement for the sake of women in their own lives that they care about?

It was when I was wracking my brains on this one that a particular character from The Matrix trilogy just came into my mind...

The Architect as Patriarchy

The Architect is the designer of the Matrix itself. He is a calculating, calm, clinical, emotionless white-bearded figure introduced in The Matrix Reloaded. He speaks in complex, hyper-rational language and is the unseen force behind the digital world in which humans unknowingly live.

The Architect is clearly meant to be a deeply familiar classic patriarchal archetype - a cinematic distillation of centuries of elite male power: a symbol of how systems have been designed by a group fo elite men mostly, around male perspectives, in ways that shape the lives of everyone else. His cold rationality often appears in our world as a supposedly benevolent neutral force but it's often used in a way that's controlling and oppressive (whether intentionally or not) within many realms - the corporate world, and certainly within engineering and physics tech cultures where I've spent over half my life.

The Architect can be seen as a perfect metaphor for patriarchal systems: He literally designs the world, much like how real-world systems (law, governance, media, education, medicine) have historically been designed by men, for men. The Matrix he creates is hegemonic, all-powerful and invisible - people live their whole lives trapped within it, without realising it. This mirrors how gender norms and male-centred structures operate: subtly but profoundly shaping daily life while appearing “neutral.”

His emotional detachment and prioritisation of logic reflects the privileging of ‘masculine-coded’ traits like reason, control, and authority, while ‘feminine’ traits like empathy, intuition, and vulnerability are absent or devalued. He is what happens when systems are built without emotional intelligence, care, or inclusion — when “neutral” actually means “male.”

The Architect: the emotionless designer of the Matrix, a deeply familiar archetype.

The Architect is a metaphor, a cinematic distillation of how patriarchy operates: as a system that appears neutral, logical, even benevolent, while in fact reflecting the priorities and perspectives of a historically dominant group.

White suit, trimmed beard, stern, calm control: he’s the visual embodiment of old-school male authority, like a professor, judge, or Victorian patriarch. His power is simply taken for granted; he doesn’t fight like the other characters, because he doesn’t need to. His dominance is structural, woven into the very code that creates the reality that humans sleep within.

This mirrors real-world dynamics, where male authority is often automatically, unconsiously treated as “default” or “neutral”, in boardrooms, governments, and media, while women or gender-diverse people are expected to justify or prove themselves. His power is programmed into the Matrix, just as real-world patriarchy is embedded in the structures we’ve inherited: in legal systems, economic frameworks, media institutions, and knowledge production. We are all unconciously programmed from our earliest years to assume that men like him have authority and expertise.

The Gender of Systems

The Architect represents the archetype of elite male authority, the system designer who appears objective, but whose objectivity is itself gendered. "Rational calm objectivity" is a lot easier when you control the world around you and some serious deficiencies in this world simply don't much affect you personally. The aesthetic of The Architect evokes centuries of power: a Victorian judge, a philosopher-king, a bureaucrat of empire - an intense focus on order, efficiency, and wholly disconnected from the emotional, intuitive, and relational elements of human life.

Crucially, The Architect builds systems without consulting the people who will live within them. The humans in the Matrix are not collaborators in their own reality — they are subjects to be managed. The Architect observes them dispassionately, like lab rats, and decides - patriarchally, not paternalistically - what is "best" for them.

This is one of the most insidious qualities of patriarchy: it positions one group as the natural decision-makers for all, based on the belief that they know better.

The traits The Architect embodies - control, logic, detachment (supposedly born of rational objectivity but often a symptom of unawareness due to privilege) - have historically been coded as masculine and positioned as superior. Meanwhile, traits more often associated with the feminine (care, empathy, vulnerability) have not only been devalued or excluded from the design of public systems; they have also been strictly controlled under patriarchy, particularly within men.

From a very early age, boys are taught to suppress vulnerability, to distance themselves from softness, to fear being perceived as weak. The result is a form of emotional discipline so deep and internalised that many men spend their adult lives trying to understand how this conditioning has shaped their sense of self, their relationships, and their understanding of others (disproportionately leading to isolation). As bell hooks explores in The Will to Change, patriarchy does not liberate men, it emotionally constrains them, often with profound consequences.

In contrast to The Architect, the Matrix universe also gives us The Oracle, a warm, maternal, intuitive foil to The Architect’s cold precision. This binary isn’t neutral either. It reinforces an old pattern: reason versus feeling, male versus female, structure versus chaos.

Real-World Echoes

The Architect isn’t based on one man, but on a lineage of power. He echoes numerous historical figures including:

  • Sigmund Freud, whose theories shaped psychology through a distinctly male lens.
  • Le Corbusier, the modernist architect whose rational cities ignored human and gendered needs.
  • Plato, whose philosopher-kings privileged rationality over lived experience.
  • Colonial administrators, governing from afar with bureaucratic detachment.
  • Early computer scientists, who designed the digital world from within overwhelmingly male paradigms.

To a greater or lesser extent, men automatically walk through our world unconsciously triggering our deeply conditioned assumptions that men are credible, rational, objective authorities, leaders. An effect which is amplified the more a man looks and sounds like The Architect - white, distinguished, elite. There are other powerful male archetypes too that automatically gain respect because we've seen them repeated so many times in our culture, such as the troubled genuis eccentric professor type - dangerous because we even assume that some volatile bad behaviour might inherently accompany genius so we should overlook it.

Unfortunately, extensive research has proven that we really do unconsciously react this way to middle/upper-class white men in general, not waiting for credibility to be proven, but assuming it exists until it's disproven, and even overlooking mistakes. White women and anyone else other than middle/upper-class white men instead get scrutinised, because we assume that they will not be credible, and that they will make mistakes - and they will instead have prove themselves over and over. The real-world personal costs of this? Pay disparities, disparities in career-progression, being disbelived by doctors, overlooked by investors, and so on...

Why This Still Matters

Even today in the UK, men’s perspectives remain centred by default:

  • Men still hold the majority of senior roles in politics, business, and academia.
  • Most editors, directors, and on-screen protagonists in UK media are male.
  • Medical research continues to focus on male bodies, leading to diagnostic gaps for women.
  • Urban design still prioritises patterns aligned with male routines and assumptions.

This isn’t about individual men. These systems already existed before any of us were born, we just inherit them - but then allow the systems to continue to replicate themselves, unless we recognise the need to consciously rebuild them.

Conclusion: We All Live in the Matrix

If someone asks “Why do we need UN Women?”, the goal is not to elevate women above men. It’s to dismantle the hidden architectures that have, for centuries, positioned one type of person as the norm, the centre of our societies and corporations and positioned everyone else as a deviation, a "niche" lesser interest.

To escape these systems, we have to become aware of and build the capability to see the code that these systems operate from, the patriarchal patterns - and start to recognise the way that these patterns persist because we unconsciously continue to power them.
