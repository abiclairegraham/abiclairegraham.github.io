The Architect and the Algorithm: What The Matrix Can Teach Us About Patriarchy

In March 2025, I had the privilege of attending CSW69 as part of a UK-based delegation engaging with the work of UN Women. One of the most common questions I’ve encountered since returning is some variation of: "Why do we still need UN Women, especially in countries like the UK? Haven’t we already achieved gender equality?"

Abi Claire Graham representing the UK at UN Women, CSW69, in March 2025.

It’s a fair question on the surface, especially in contexts where legal rights are broadly equal. But the answer lies not in legislation, but in structures: in who designs the systems we live within, whose needs are centred, and whose voices are still considered the default.

UN Women exists because, globally, women and girls continue to experience systemic inequality. From underrepresentation in leadership and overrepresentation in unpaid care work, to higher exposure to gender-based violence and economic vulnerability, the evidence remains stark. In the UK, too, structural imbalances persist: men still dominate most decision-making roles across politics, business, media, and tech. Despite progress, gender remains one of the most consistent axes of power imbalance.

Which brings us, unexpectedly, to The Matrix.

The Architect as Patriarchy

Enter The Architect — the calculating, white-bearded figure introduced in The Matrix Reloaded as the designer of the Matrix itself. He’s calm, clinical, emotionless. He speaks in complex, hyper-rational language. He is the unseen force behind the digital world in which humans unknowingly live.

The Architect: the emotionless designer of the Matrix, a deeply familiar archetype.

In many ways, The Architect is not just a character. He is a metaphor — a cinematic distillation of how patriarchy operates: as a system that appears neutral, logical, even benevolent, while in fact reflecting the priorities and perspectives of a historically dominant group.

He doesn’t need to shout or coerce. His power is embedded in the code — just as real-world patriarchy is embedded in the structures we’ve inherited: in legal systems, economic frameworks, media institutions, and knowledge production.

The Gender of Systems

The Architect represents the archetype of elite male authority — the system designer who appears objective, but whose objectivity is itself gendered. His aesthetic evokes centuries of power: a Victorian judge, a philosopher-king, a bureaucrat of empire. His Matrix is sleek, ordered, efficient — and wholly disconnected from the emotional, intuitive, and relational elements of human life.

Crucially, he builds systems without consulting the people who will live within them. The humans in the Matrix are not collaborators in their own reality — they are subjects to be managed. He observes them dispassionately, like lab rats, and decides — patriarchally, not paternalistically — what is best for them.

This is one of the most insidious qualities of patriarchy: it positions one group as the natural decision-makers for all, based on the belief that they know better.

The traits The Architect embodies — control, logic, distance — have historically been coded as masculine and positioned as superior. Meanwhile, traits more often associated with the feminine — care, empathy, vulnerability — have not only been devalued or excluded from the design of public systems; they have also been strictly controlled under patriarchy, particularly within men.

From a very early age, boys are taught to suppress vulnerability, to distance themselves from softness, to fear being perceived as weak. The result is a form of emotional discipline so deep and internalised that many men spend their adult lives trying to understand how this conditioning has shaped their sense of self, their relationships, and their understanding of others. As bell hooks explores in The Will to Change, patriarchy does not liberate men — it emotionally constrains them, often with profound consequences.

In contrast, the Matrix universe also gives us The Oracle — warm, maternal, intuitive — a foil to The Architect’s cold precision. This binary isn’t neutral either. It reinforces an old pattern: reason versus feeling, male versus female, structure versus chaos.

Real-World Echoes

The Architect isn’t based on one man, but on a lineage of power. He echoes:

  • Sigmund Freud, whose theories shaped psychology through a distinctly male lens.
  • Le Corbusier, the modernist architect whose rational cities ignored human and gendered needs.
  • Plato, whose philosopher-kings privileged rationality over lived experience.
  • Colonial administrators, governing from afar with bureaucratic detachment.
  • Early computer scientists, who designed the digital world from within overwhelmingly male paradigms.

The Architect is what happens when systems are designed without the input of those they govern — when logic is allowed to masquerade as neutrality, and when inclusion is seen as optional.

Why This Still Matters

Even today in the UK, men’s perspectives remain centred by default:

  • Men still hold the majority of senior roles in politics, business, and academia.
  • Most editors, directors, and on-screen protagonists in UK media are male.
  • Medical research continues to focus on male bodies, leading to diagnostic gaps for women.
  • Urban design still prioritises patterns aligned with male routines and assumptions.

This isn’t about individual men. It’s about how the system continues to replicate itself — unless we recognise the need to consciously rebuild it.

Conclusion: We All Live in the Matrix

To ask “Why UN Women?” is, in some ways, to miss the point. The goal is not to elevate women above men — it’s to dismantle the hidden architectures that have, for centuries, positioned one type of person as the norm and everyone else as deviation.

The Architect may live in a fictional universe, but his real-world equivalents are everywhere — not in individual men, but in the inherited logics of our institutions. His control is quiet, embedded, systemic.

And as The Matrix teaches us, the first step to freedom is seeing the code.

The more I think about it, the more it seems that The Architect is clearly meant to be a deeply familiar classic patriarchal archetype - a cinematic distillation of centuries of elite male power: a symbol of how systems have been designed by a group fo elite men mostly, around male perspectives, in ways that shape the lives of everyone else. His cold rationality often appears in our world as a supposedly benevolent neutral force but it's often used in a way that's controlling and oppressive (whether intentionally or not) within many realms - the corporate world, and certainly within engineering and physics tech cultures where I've spent over half my life.
