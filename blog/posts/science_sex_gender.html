What Mainstream Science Actually Says About Sex, Gender, and Trans People

This post is my personal “cheat sheet” of mainstream positions from foremost scientific and professional bodies – the kind that regulate healthcare, set clinical guidelines, and write textbooks.

A simple reference for how major scientific organisations define sex and gender, plus a myth-vs-reality section on common misconceptions.

When people talk about sex, gender, and trans identities, they often say things like “science says…” – but which science? Which organisations? And what do they actually say, in writing, on their own websites?

It has two parts:

  • A short and simple list of common misconceptions vs scientific positions
  • A table of major organisations and how they define sex and gender, with links
High-level flow of how scientific consensus forms
1

Researchers collect evidence

Scientists run studies, publish peer-reviewed papers, and argue/debate/discuss with each other about what the data means.

2

Reviews and guidelines are written

Professional bodies (like the APA, AMA, WHO, WPATH, AAP, BPS) review the evidence in a systematic, structured way and write summaries, position statements, and guidelines.

3

Organisations adopt official positions

Those documents become the “house view” of the organisation, shaping training, ethics codes, recommendations and clinical practice.

4

Definitions and policies get updated

As more data comes in, organisations revise their policies in line with latest scientific consensus. This is why many have updated language around sex, gender, and transgender health in the last decade.

Part 1 — Common Misconceptions vs Scientific Positions

These are some of the most common misunderstandings, next to what mainstream scientific and professional bodies actually say - and links to position-statements or references from those bodies which corroborate this can be found in the table further down this page.

Misconception 1: “Sex is strictly binary.”

Scientific position: Sex is biological, but not perfectly binary in practice. Most people are classified as male or female, but there is natural variation in chromosomes, gonads, hormone patterns, and anatomy (for example, intersex variations). Modern biology and medicine explicitly acknowledge this complexity rather than pretending it doesn’t exist.

Misconception 2: “Gender is just another word for sex.”

Scientific position: Every major organisation in this table separates them. Very roughly:

  • Sex = biological traits (chromosomes, hormones, anatomy, etc.).
  • Gender = social and psychological roles, norms, and categories.
  • Gender identity = a person’s internal sense of their own gender.

They interact, but they’re not the same construct, and you can’t collapse them into one word without losing important information.

Misconception 3: “Gender identity is a new political invention.”

Scientific position: The idea that people have an internal sense of their gender has been around in psychology, psychiatry, and sexology since the mid-20th century. What’s changed is the language, the quality of the data, and the level of social awareness, not the underlying human experience.

Misconception 4: “Being transgender is a mental disorder.”

Scientific position: Major bodies (WHO, APA, BPS, AMA, AAP, WPATH) do not treat being transgender itself as a disorder. Some trans and gender-diverse people experience distress because of the mismatch between their body, social treatment, or legal status and their identity, or because of stigma and discrimination. The distress is the problem, not the existence of the identity.

Misconception 5: “Biology contradicts transgender identities.”

Scientific position: Biology describes sex traits. Gender identity is a psychological and social phenomenon. Biologists do not define who is a man or woman in the social or psychological sense; that’s not what the discipline is for. So “biology vs gender identity” is mostly a category error — they’re talking about different things.

Misconception 6: “Trans women aren’t women / trans men aren’t men in science.”

Scientific position: In psychology, medicine, sociology and public health, gender categories are defined in terms of gender identity and social role. When these organisations talk about “women” in gender-identity contexts, they include trans women; when they talk about “men”, they include trans men. Biology, again, generally doesn’t try to define gender categories at all.

Misconception 7: “Trans people think they have different biology and that feelings trump reality.”

A very common misunderstanding is that trans people “believe” they are biologically the opposite sex, or that they think their feelings magically overwrite physical reality. That isn’t what gender identity is, and it isn’t how mainstream science or healthcare understands trans people.

Most trans people are perfectly aware of their bodies. A trans woman who was assigned male at birth usually knows she went through a male-typical puberty; a trans man knows he was assigned female at birth. Trans people do not need a lecture on chromosomes or anatomy – they live in their bodies every day. What they are describing is not a belief about their past biology, but their current and enduring sense of self and the social role they belong in.

In other words:

  • Biology describes bodies. Sex traits (chromosomes, hormones, organs) are part of physical reality and are not “denied” by having a gender identity.
  • Gender identity describes the kind of person someone is. It’s a psychological and social reality about who they are in the world: which pronouns fit, which spaces and roles make sense, which category (man, woman, nonbinary, etc.) they belong in.
  • Transition is about alignment. Social and medical transition are ways of bringing someone’s outward life (name, pronouns, documents, sometimes hormones or surgeries) into better alignment with their internal identity, not ways of pretending their body was always different.

So it isn’t “feelings versus reality”. Gender identity is part of reality – a stable psychological fact about a person, just like sexual orientation or personality. Mainstream psychology and medicine recognise that respecting this aspect of reality (by using correct names, pronouns, and providing appropriate care) leads to much better mental and physical health outcomes, whereas denying it does not change anyone’s body – it only increases distress.

Misconception 8: “You can’t change biology.”

This statement is often used as if it settles the discussion – but it oversimplifies what “biology” actually means. Biology is not one single thing. It is a collection of systems: hormones, organs, secondary sex characteristics, gene expression, physical structure, and psychological traits. And many of these can be changed, often profoundly.

Modern medicine cannot rewrite someone’s developmental history, and we cannot currently replace a Y chromosome with an X chromosome. But this does not mean that a person’s biology is fixed in all meaningful ways. In fact, hormone replacement therapy (HRT) produces major, measurable, medically-documented biological changes throughout the body.

  • Hormones are one of the most powerful biological regulators we have. Changing hormone levels affects skin, fat distribution, muscle mass, bone turnover, connective tissue, hair growth, metabolism, libido, emotional processing, and more. Testosterone, for example, reduces tear-duct sensitivity and suppresses the crying reflex; estrogen reshapes fat storage and softens connective tissue. These are real biological changes, not “just feelings”.
  • Secondary sex characteristics are highly hormone-dependent. With HRT, many of these characteristics shift significantly. This is why mainstream endocrinology treats HRT as legitimate medical care for trans adults.
  • Chromosomes matter far less in adults than people think. Chromosomes largely determine how the body develops in the womb and during puberty. But in adulthood, their ongoing role is limited. Day-to-day physiology is driven much more by hormones, environment, behaviour, and gene expression than by the chromosomes themselves.
  • Chromosome editing is an active research field. Scientists can already modify small regions of chromosomes, remove or silence genes, or add synthetic ones using technologies such as CRISPR. What we cannot yet do is fully swap an entire Y for an X. That is a deep technical challenge involving thousands of genes and regulatory regions. But importantly, trans healthcare does not require this.

So the relevant scientific point is not “biology can’t change” — it’s that certain kinds of biology can change dramatically, and others don’t need to change for transition to be valid or effective.

A trans person does not need different chromosomes to live healthily as their gender. What matters for well-being is alignment between identity, social role, and the aspects of biology that are changeable: hormones, physical features, and lived experience. These are the factors that affect someone’s life, health, and functioning — and these are the factors medical science can meaningfully support.

Misconception 9: “Science is totally divided on all of this.”

Scientific position: There are debates about details (for example, best clinical pathways, language, or how to structure services), and there are political fights around how to implement policy. But across the big mainstream organisations, there is a striking level of agreement that:

  • Sex is biological and somewhat messy in real life.
  • Gender is social and psychological.
  • Gender identity is real.
  • Trans people exist, and trans-inclusive care and social recognition are important for health.

Part 2 — How Major Organisations Define Sex and Gender

This table pulls together a quick reference for several large, mainstream organisations. It’s not exhaustive, but it gives a flavour of the overall landscape.

World Health Organization (WHO)
(Founded in 1948)		 Biological traits such as chromosomes, reproductive anatomy and hormones; the WHO also acknowledges natural variation (including intersex). The socially constructed characteristics of women, men, girls and boys (norms, behaviours, roles and relationships), varying across cultures and over time. A person’s internal and individual experience of gender, which may or may not match their sex assigned at birth. UN specialised agency and global public health authority. Publishes the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), used by health systems worldwide. WHO – Gender and health
American Psychological Association (APA)
(Founded in 1892)		 Biological attributes such as anatomy, chromosomes, hormones (often grouped under “sex assigned at birth”). The attitudes, feelings, and behaviours that a given culture associates with being male or female. A person’s deeply felt, internal sense of being male, female, a blend of both, neither, or another gender. Largest psychological organisation in the world. Sets widely used standards for research ethics, clinical practice, and training in psychology. APA – Understanding transgender people, gender identity and gender expression
British Psychological Society (BPS)
(Founded in 1901)		 Biological classification into male, female, and intersex categories, based on anatomy and chromosomes. A social and psychological construct covering roles, expectations, and identities related to being a man, woman, or other gender. A person’s internal sense of their own gender, which may or may not align with sex assigned at birth. Main professional and regulatory body for psychologists in the UK. Accredits training and issues ethics and practice guidelines. BPS – Guidelines for Psychologists Working with GSRD
American Medical Association (AMA)
(Founded in 1847)		 Biological characteristics such as reproductive anatomy, chromosomes, and hormone levels. A person’s deeply felt sense of being male, female, or another gender, which may or may not correspond to sex assigned at birth. The internal experience of gender that guides how a person understands themselves and wishes to be recognised socially. Largest association of physicians and medical students in the US. Influential in medical ethics, health policy, and clinical guidelines. AMA – Policies on LGBTQ issues (including gender identity)
WPATH (World Professional Association for Transgender Health)
(Founded in 1979)		 Biological traits such as chromosomes, gonads, hormones, and anatomy (mainly in relation to health care, rather than to define gender). A multidimensional construct that includes gender identity, roles, and expression, in interaction with social and cultural factors. A person’s deeply felt, internal sense of gender (for example, man, woman, nonbinary), which may or may not align with sex assigned at birth. International professional association whose Standards of Care are widely used as the primary clinical reference for transgender and gender-diverse health worldwide. WPATH – Standards of Care Version 8
Biology (mainstream scientific consensus) Sex is treated as a multidimensional biological trait: chromosomes, gonads, hormone patterns, reproductive anatomy, and secondary sex characteristics, with recognised natural variation (including intersex). Generally not defined; most biological disciplines treat gender as a social and psychological construct rather than a biological one. Also not defined within biology; gender identity is usually left to psychology and social sciences. Authority comes from cumulative peer-reviewed research, scientific societies, and consensus across multiple fields, rather than a single central organisation. Representative overviews can be found in review articles in journals such as Nature Reviews and similar; there isn’t one official “biology” website.
American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP)
(Founded in 1930)		 Sex assigned at birth based on observed anatomy and, where relevant, chromosomes. A social and psychological concept related to roles, expectations, and identities associated with being a boy, girl, man, woman, or other gender. An individual’s internal sense of their gender, including transgender and gender-diverse identities. Largest professional organisation of pediatricians in the US. Issues widely followed policy statements on child and adolescent health. AAP – Reaffirms gender-affirming care policy

Wrapping up

None of this means science is a closed matter and that no further developments in understanding will occur, or that there are no disagreements. But if someone says “mainstream science” is fundamentally hostile to trans people, that doesn’t match the current evidence-based positions of the overseeing organisations in each field.

Having these links and definitions in one place makes it much easier to check claims like “science says X” and see what the evidence-based positions of major authorites in different disciplines - WHO, APA, BPS, AMA, AAP, WPATH - actually are.
